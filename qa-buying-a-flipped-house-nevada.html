Buying a flipped house: how to tell craftsmanship from lipstick

Flips aren't the enemy — bad flips are, and they're detectable: the diligence that separates a professionally renovated home from a lipstick job runs through the permit trail (was the electrical, plumbing, and structural work inspected, or just drywalled over?), the speed-and-scope story (closed six weeks ago and 'fully renovated' invites arithmetic), and inspection attention aimed at what paint hides — panels, plumbing generations, roof reality, and the junctions where new meets old. NV Ball Real Estate Group buys flips happily when the work is real; here's how we tell.

Inspect past the paint

The permit trail first

County records show what was permitted and finalized; the gap between the listing's renovation story and the permit record is the single most revealing document in a flip purchase. Unpermitted systems work isn't automatically disqualifying — but it reprices the house and the risk, knowingly.

The arithmetic read

Purchase date, purchase price (public record), scope claimed, and time elapsed — four facts that tell you whether the budget could have bought the renovation described. Six weeks and a full-gut story don't multiply; honest flips have timelines that math.

Flip-specific inspection

We point inspectors at what staging hides: the panel and its era, plumbing generations at the junctions, the roof (painted fascia isn't a new roof), insulation reality, and workmanship at corners literal and figurative. Plus warranty posture: real renovators stand behind work; lipstick artists have already moved on. The inspection guide pairs with this page.

Straight answers

Is it OK to buy a flipped house?

Yes — real renovations are a legitimate shortcut. The sort: permit trail vs. the story, timeline-budget arithmetic, inspection past the paint. Good flips survive all three checks; lipstick fails somewhere.

What are the red flags in a flipped house?

No permits behind the claims, impossible timelines, 'all new' contradicted by the panel or roof, strategic-only fresh paint, a ghost-entity seller. One flag: look harder. Several: reprice or pass.

Does a flip's unpermitted work kill the deal?

It reprices, not auto-kills: unknown quality, lender/insurer wrinkles, your future disclosure. Paths: retroactive permits, a risk-covering discount, or walking. 'Knowingly' is the only way forward.
