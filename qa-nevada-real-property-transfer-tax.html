Nevada's real property transfer tax, explained

Every Nevada deed recording triggers the real property transfer tax (RPTT) — calculated on the sale price at a rate set per $500 of value, varying modestly by county (Washoe's rate includes a county addition to the state base). Custom in Northern Nevada typically has the seller paying it, though like most closing costs it's negotiable in the contract. A set of statutory exemptions covers transfers that aren't really sales — between certain family members, into your own trust, divorce divisions — and knowing them saves real money on estate and entity moves. Escrow calculates the exact figure; here's the working understanding.

How it works

Rate per $500 of declared value, recorded with the deed — the mechanics are simple and the county recorder is unforgiving about them. Exact current rates per county: escrow quotes them live (printed rates go stale; the structure here doesn't).

Who pays

Northern Nevada custom: seller — but it's a negotiable line like the rest of the closing-cost split, and competitive dynamics sometimes move it. The contract's word beats custom's; we write it explicitly.

The exemptions that matter

Statutory exemptions cover transfers without real consideration: certain family transfers, moves into your own revocable trust, divorce-decree divisions, and others. Estate planning and entity transfers should be structured with the exemption list in view — a title-officer conversation we set up routinely.

Straight answers

How much is the transfer tax when selling a house in Nevada?

Per-$500-of-price, county-varying rate — escrow quotes the exact current number. Custom here: seller pays; contracts can move it.

Are any transfers exempt from Nevada's transfer tax?

Yes — non-sale transfers: certain family deeds, your own revocable trust, divorce divisions, more. Check the list before any estate or entity recording; the title officer confirms.

Does the transfer tax apply when I put my house in a trust?

Your own revocable trust: standard exemption — routine, not costly. Entities: more nuanced; exemption analysis before recording, with the title officer and your attorney.
