For sellers · Getting ahead of surprises

Should I get a pre-listing inspection in Nevada?

Sometimes — and it's worth deciding deliberately. A pre-listing inspection lets you fix or price problems on your schedule instead of negotiating them under deadline, and it feeds Nevada's seller-disclosure form with confident answers. The honest counterweights: you'll pay for it, and what it finds, you now know — which means disclosing it. NV Ball Real Estate Group recommends it for older homes, deferred-maintenance histories, and tight timelines; for newer, well-kept homes we often skip it.

Get a straight recommendation

When it pays

Older homes, DIY histories, long ownership without major service, and sellers who can't absorb a mid-escrow renegotiation. Knowing first means fixing at bid prices, not concession prices.

The disclosure reality

What an inspection finds, Nevada's disclosure framework expects you to share. That's a feature, not a bug — disclosed-and-priced beats discovered-and-renegotiated — but it's why the decision is deliberate, and the disclosure guide is this page's sibling.

When to skip

Newer construction, recently serviced systems, and homes we've walked without red flags. The buyer will inspect anyway; paying to learn what you already know helps no one.

Straight answers

Is a pre-listing inspection worth it?

Older home, DIY history, or a tight timeline — usually yes: fix at bid prices, not concession prices. Newer and well-kept — often skip it; the buyer inspects anyway.

Do I have to disclose what it finds?

Yes, in practice — known conditions belong on the disclosure. That IS the strategy: disclosed-and-priced rarely kills a deal; discovered-in-escrow reprices it.

What does one cost and who do you use?

Varies by size and scope. We'll refer inspectors we trust — and tell you first whether your home needs one at all. The consult is free.
