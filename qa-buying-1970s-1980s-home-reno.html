Buying a '70s or '80s house here: the era guide

A huge share of Reno's established neighborhoods — the Old Southwest's later streets, Hidden Valley, parts of Sparks, the first Caughlin phases — were built in the 1970s and '80s, and these homes deserve era-literate diligence: what the decades built well (real lots, mature trees, solid framing, floor plans a wall-removal away from modern), and the known-issues list inspectors check by vintage — electrical panels from brands with documented problems (certain era panels are automatic insurance conversations; your inspector names names), aluminum branch wiring in some early-'70s builds, galvanized or early-generation plumbing reaching end of life, original windows and insulation from pre-code-evolution years (the retrofit order exists for exactly these), and the asbestos-lead-by-era file for anything pre-1980. None of it vetoes; all of it prices. Here's the decade-by-decade read.

Inspect by era

What these eras got right

Lot sizes modern subdivisions quietly shrank, mature landscaping doing decades of paid work (shade, windbreaks — the high-desert premium), framing and rooflines that renovate willingly, and locations that were the edge of town then and the established middle now. The era's homes are the region's renovation canvas — bought right, they're the value play established neighborhoods offer.

The known-issues list

By system: electrical (era panels with documented failure histories get flagged by every competent inspector and many insurers — replacement is routine and priceable; aluminum branch wiring in some early-'70s homes has known remediation paths), plumbing (galvanized supply and early plastics age out; repipes are common era projects), envelope (original single-pane windows and thin insulation — the retrofit ROI order applies), and the pre-1980 asbestos-lead protocols for remodel planning. Every item is knowable in inspection and priceable in negotiation; surprises only happen to buyers who skipped the era homework.

The negotiation translation

Era issues are leverage when documented and priced: inspection findings convert to credits or price adjustments against real bid numbers (not panic), insurance conversations happen before contingencies expire (some carriers care about panels and roofs more than others — shop accordingly), and the renovation math runs honestly against the new-build alternative. We've negotiated these homes for years; the era is a feature priced correctly.

Straight answers

What should I look for buying a 1970s or 1980s house?

The list: era electrical panels (inspectors name names; replacement priceable), early-'70s aluminum branch wiring, aging galvanized plumbing, original windows/insulation, pre-1980 asbestos-lead protocols. All knowable, all priceable — against real lots, mature trees and willing floor plans.

Are older homes in Reno worth buying?

Often — lots, trees and locations new builds can't make, priced against renewal-age systems that inspection documents. Price the list going in; don't discover it.

Will insurance cover a house with an old electrical panel?

Carrier- and panel-dependent: documented-problem brands trigger some insurers' requirements. Have the insurance conversation inside your contingency window; price replacement into negotiation.
