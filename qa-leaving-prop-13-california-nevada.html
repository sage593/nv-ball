Relocation · The golden handcuffs

Leaving Prop 13 behind: the California seller's honest math

The hesitation has a name — golden handcuffs — and it's legitimate: decades-held California homes carry Prop 13 assessed values far below market (taxes capped near a long-ago purchase price plus limited annual growth), Prop 19's base-transfer provisions for eligible homeowners work only within California (move to Nevada and the old base stays behind — there's no interstate transfer), so the move genuinely trades away a protected tax position. The other side of the ledger, honestly: Nevada's property taxes run on its own system with its own abatement caps on annual increases (the full comparison), there's no state income tax on the other end (the complete picture), and the sale itself raises capital-gains questions with their own planning. The right answer is arithmetic, not folklore — run with your CPA, grounded here.

Run the whole-ledger math

What you're actually giving up

Prop 13's deal: assessed value anchored near your purchase price, annual growth capped low, taxes that became a smaller share of reality every year you held — and Prop 19's over-55/disabled/disaster base transfers move that protection only to another California home. Cross the state line and the base is gone; that's the real cost, and pretending otherwise serves nobody.

What Nevada hands back

Its own tax architecture: property taxes computed on Nevada's system with statutory abatement caps limiting annual increases on primary residences (different mechanics, same spirit of protection — details in the comparison), no state income tax touching salary, retirement income or investment gains going forward, and the purchase-time notes every mover should read (the abatement reset). The ledgers differ in shape, which is why single-line comparisons mislead.

The whole-ledger method

Not 'property tax here versus there' but everything: the old base's annual value against Nevada's income-tax zero (for working and retirement income both), the sale's capital-gains treatment (planned, not discovered), and the housing-arithmetic difference doing its quiet work. Some households' math says stay; plenty says go — we've watched both conclusions reached honestly, and the CPA-plus-live-numbers conversation is how. We'll bring the Nevada half.

Straight answers

Do I lose my Prop 13 tax basis if I move to Nevada?

Yes — the base stays in California (Prop 19 transfers work only in-state). But run the whole ledger: Nevada's abatement caps, zero state income tax, and the sale's capital-gains planning, with a CPA.

Is it worth giving up Prop 13 to move to Nevada?

Arithmetic: the base's annual savings vs. the income-tax zero plus housing math. Low base + modest income can say stay; most working and retirement ledgers say go. CPA referees.

Does Nevada have anything like Prop 13?

In spirit: statutory abatement caps limit annual increases (lowest for primary residences) on Nevada's own assessment mechanics. Read the purchase-time reset notes; assessors publish specifics.
