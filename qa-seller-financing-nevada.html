For buyers & sellers · The private-loan path

How does seller financing work in Nevada?

In a seller-financed (carry-back) deal, the seller plays bank: the buyer pays over time under a recorded note and deed of trust instead of (or alongside) institutional financing. It solves real problems — buyers banks won't fit, sellers wanting income streams or faster sales, free-and-clear properties meeting rate-weary buyers — and it carries real requirements: professional documentation, regulatory rules when the buyer will occupy the home, and honest underwriting that most handshake versions skip. NV Ball Real Estate Group structures these with the attorneys and servicers they need; here's the straight map.

Structure it properly

When it genuinely fits

Sellers who own free-and-clear and want income plus a faster sale; buyers with strong down payments but bank-unfriendly profiles (self-employed, recent credit events); land and unique properties banks price poorly. The overlap is real — and smaller than the internet implies.

The guardrails

A recorded note and deed of trust drafted by a real estate attorney, professional loan servicing (never checks-to-a-mailbox), title and escrow run normally, and — when the buyer will occupy — compliance rules around owner-financed residential loans that genuinely matter. We assemble this team on every carry-back.

The honest risks

Sellers: you're underwriting a borrower — demand real financials and a down payment that means it, and understand foreclosure is your remedy if it sours. Buyers: balloon terms need an exit plan before signing. Deals structured to skip these truths are the ones that end in stories.

Straight answers

What is seller financing and is it legal in Nevada?

Legal, established, recorded: note + deed of trust, payments over time, foreclosure as the seller's remedy. Done right: attorney documents, professional servicing, normal escrow, occupancy-rule compliance. The handshake version is the cautionary tale.

Why would a seller finance the sale?

Income at a negotiated rate, secured by a known property; a wider buyer pool; sometimes a premium; CPA-relevant installment timing. Fits free-and-clear sellers who don't need the lump sum — real, but specific.

What should a buyer check before accepting seller financing?

What a bank would force: market-tested terms, a real plan for any balloon, professional servicing, clean recorded title, attorney's read. It's a tool — never a shortcut past diligence.
