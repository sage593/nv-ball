For co-buyers · Partnership on paper

Buying a house with someone you're not married to: do it right

Unmarried co-buying works — partners, friends, siblings, parents-and-kids buy together all the time — and the difference between fine and disaster is paperwork most skip: how title is held (joint tenancy's survivorship versus tenants-in-common's defined shares), a co-ownership agreement covering money and exits (who pays what, what happens on a breakup, buyout mechanics, forced-sale rights), and honest acknowledgment that marriage law's default protections don't apply to you — your contract is your protection. NV Ball Real Estate Group closes these regularly, and insists on the grown-up conversation first.

Structure our purchase

The title decision

Joint tenancy: equal shares, survivorship — one owner dies, the other inherits automatically. Tenants in common: defined shares (50/50 or 70/30), each willable to anyone. The right answer follows your actual relationship and contributions — and it's an attorney-blessed choice, not a checkbox default.

The agreement that saves friendships

Before closing, on paper: contribution shares, payment responsibilities, repair-decision rules, what happens when one wants out (buyout formula, timeline, forced-sale fallback), breakup and death scenarios. Couples find this unromantic; former co-owners in litigation find it genius. We'll point you to attorneys who draft them routinely.

The lender reality

Both incomes and both credit profiles walk into underwriting — the weaker file prices the loan — and both names on the note means both fully liable regardless of your private split. Exits later mean refinancing or selling; negotiating the purchase is the easy part, honestly.

Straight answers

Can two unmarried people buy a house together in Nevada?

Routinely — with deliberate structure: title chosen on purpose (survivorship vs. defined shares), a co-ownership agreement covering money and exits, and clear eyes on full joint liability. Marriage law won't protect you; your contract will.

What happens if we break up or one of us wants out?

Whatever the agreement says — buyout formula, sale timeline, refinance mechanics. Without one: worst-moment negotiation with partition litigation as the backstop. Cheap before closing; a fortune after.

Whose credit is used when co-buying?

Both — priced off the weaker file, usually. Sometimes one strong borrower beats two mixed ones (ownership handled separately, with counsel). Lender runs both versions first.
