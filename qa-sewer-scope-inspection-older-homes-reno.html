Sewer scope inspections: the older-home diligence that pays

The sewer lateral — the pipe running from the house to the street — is the most expensive component your general inspection doesn't examine: it's outside the inspector's scope, buried, and in older neighborhoods (the University District, Old Southwest, Midtown's bungalow blocks, mid-century Sparks) it may be clay, cast iron, or the infamous mid-century fiber conduit, all with root intrusion and collapse as their retirement plans. A camera scope costs a fraction of one repair and turns the gamble into information. NV Ball Real Estate Group orders scopes on older homes as near-standard practice — here's why and when.

Scope before you own it

Why the lateral is special

It's yours (homeowner responsibility to the street in most cases), it's buried (failures announce themselves badly), and replacement runs serious five figures when streets and trees get involved. No other single component combines invisibility with that price tag — which is exactly what cameras were invented for.

The eras that matter

Pre-war and early post-war neighborhoods ran clay and cast iron (roots love the joints); certain mid-century years used bituminous fiber conduit with a known deformation destiny; and even newer homes near big trees earn a look. Your home's era and street trees set the risk; we'll tell you honestly when the scope is paranoia versus prudence.

What results actually mean

Clean scope: peace of mind, cheap. Roots and minor offsets: maintenance items, priced and negotiable. Bellies, breaks, or collapse: real money — now a negotiation with evidence instead of a post-closing ambush. Every outcome beats not knowing.

Straight answers

Is a sewer scope inspection worth it when buying an older house?

In older neighborhoods — emphatically: buried, excluded from general inspections, yours to the street, five figures to replace. The camera costs a fraction and converts the gamble into information.

Which Reno homes most need a sewer scope?

Era + trees: pre-war and early post-war blocks, the mid-century fiber-conduit years, and anything under mature street trees. New suburbs: rarely. Century homes: almost always.

The scope found problems — now what?

Negotiate with evidence: minor findings → maintenance or modest credits; serious ones → bids and real negotiation, footage as exhibit A. Before closing: leverage. After: your bill.
