For sellers & buyers · The recovery

The sale fell through: what happens next

Deals die — inspection standoffs, financing failures, appraisal gaps, cold feet — and what happens next has a playbook for both sides. For sellers: the earnest-money question resolves by the contract's contingency terms (a buyer canceling inside a valid contingency typically gets the deposit back; one canceling outside them typically doesn't — escrow follows the paper), the home goes back on market with a 'back on market' story that deserves managing (we front-run the question buyers will ask), and backup offers, where they exist, become suddenly relevant. For buyers: a deposit returned per contingencies, lessons priced in, and a search that resumes wiser. The second launch usually succeeds — here's how to run it.

Run the second launch right

The earnest-money resolution

The contract decides: cancellation inside a valid contingency (inspection, financing, appraisal) typically returns the deposit; cancellation outside them typically forfeits it — and escrow releases funds per the signed terms, not per anyone's frustration. The earnest-money guide covers the fine print; disputes are rare when contracts were written cleanly.

The seller's second launch

Back-on-market listings get asked one question — 'what happened?' — and the answer deserves front-running: a financing failure is the buyer's story, not the house's (say so); an inspection standoff means deciding whether to fix, credit or disclose-and-price before relisting. Honest, fast repositioning beats hoping nobody asks; our relaunch includes exactly that narrative work.

The buyer's recovery

Deposit resolution per the contingencies, then the useful audit: did the deal die from something knowable earlier (financing stretch, inspection surprise, appraisal gap)? The second search runs better armed — pre-approval hardened, budgets honest, and the escrow conduct rules followed to the letter.

Straight answers

What happens when a home sale falls through?

Reset per contract: earnest money follows the contingency terms, the seller relists with the story managed (financing failure = buyer's story, not the house's), backups activate, the buyer resumes wiser. Fast, honest second launches usually succeed.

Does a seller keep the earnest money when a buyer backs out?

Only on cancellations outside valid contingencies; inside them the deposit typically returns. Escrow follows the signed paper, not frustration.

Is a back-on-market listing damaged goods?

Only if the question gets dodged. Financing failure: say it plainly. Inspection issue: fix, credit or disclose before relisting. Managed fast and honestly, they sell routinely.
