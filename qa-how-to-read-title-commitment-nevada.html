For buyers · The document everyone skims

How to read your preliminary title commitment (and what actually matters)

The title commitment is the document everyone receives and nobody reads — and its Schedule B exceptions list is where your future property rights actually live: the routine entries (utility easements, standard CC&Rs, tax liens pending proration) coexist with the occasional flag (access easements across your yard, mineral-rights reservations, un-reconveyed old deeds of trust, HOA super-priority notices). The read takes twenty minutes with a translator; skipping it converts week-one information into week-four drama or post-closing surprise. NV Ball Real Estate Group reads every commitment beside our buyers — here's the field guide to the document.

Read mine with me

The document's anatomy

Schedule A: the deal's facts — parties, property, proposed insurance. Schedule B: the exceptions — everything the title policy won't insure against, which is the list that governs your ownership. Requirements: what must clear before closing (payoffs, document fixes). The exceptions schedule is where the twenty minutes go.

Routine versus flag

Routine: utility easements along lot lines, recorded CC&Rs (read separately), current-year taxes, standard mineral severances in some areas. Flags worth conversation: access easements crossing usable yard, un-reconveyed old loans (fixable, pre-closing), unexpected liens, and anything your plans collide with — the easement guide goes deeper.

What to do with a flag

Nothing dramatic: most flags resolve by escrow routine (payoff demands, reconveyance hunts), some by negotiation, a few by walking — and all of them resolve better in week one than week four. The title officer answers questions for free; we ask them with you, every commitment, as standard practice.

Straight answers

What should I look for in a preliminary title report?

Schedule B, the exceptions — the list governing your actual ownership. Routine: utility easements, CC&Rs, taxes. Flags: yard-crossing easements, un-reconveyed old loans, surprise liens. Twenty translated minutes in week one; zero week-four drama.

What's a title requirement versus an exception?

Requirements: cleared before closing (escrow's to-do list). Exceptions: survive closing as your policy's limits (your forever list). Read the forever list.

I found something concerning in the exceptions — now what?

In order: title officer explains (free; most scary language is routine), escrow fixes the fixable, negotiation prices the rest, your contingency covers the walk. Earlier reading = everything works better.
