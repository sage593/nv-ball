For buyers & owners · The invisible question

Should I worry about radon in a Northern Nevada home?

Worth testing for, yes — panicking over, no. Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas from the region's geology, parts of Northern Nevada test elevated more often than the national picture, and the only way to know any specific house is a test — radon varies house to house, even next door. The good news is proportionate: testing is cheap, mitigation is a solved problem (a fan-and-pipe system installed by pros), and homes with mitigation systems sell normally. NV Ball Real Estate Group treats radon as routine diligence, not drama.

Make it routine diligence

Why here

Granite-adjacent geology means parts of the region test elevated more often than average. County health and the university extension run awareness programs for a reason — it's a known local topic, handled sensibly.

Testing, sensibly

Short-term tests are inexpensive and fit inside an inspection period; winter closed-house conditions give truer readings. We fold it into escrow diligence as standard practice on request — and recommend it.

If it's elevated

Mitigation systems (sub-slab depressurization — a sealed pipe and fan) are a solved, inspectable fix installed by certified pros. In transactions, elevated results become a negotiation item like any repair: priced, fixed, documented. Homes with systems sell normally.

Straight answers

Is radon a problem in Northern Nevada?

A known regional topic — our geology tests elevated more often than average, and testing is officially recommended. Frame it sensibly: no house is knowable without a (cheap) test, and elevated has a routine, inspectable fix.

Should I test during a home purchase?

Yes — cheap, fits the inspection window, and turns an invisible unknown into peace of mind or a fixable repair item. Sellers: test pre-listing and take the topic off the table.

Does radon mitigation hurt resale?

Practically the opposite — a documented system pre-answers the question. The unaddressed unknown is what hurts. Routine handling beats week-three surprises.
