Easements and boundary disputes in Nevada: what you need to know

Two truths start every boundary conversation: easements run with the land (the utility's right, the neighbor's driveway access, the ditch company's maintenance strip — recorded ones bind you regardless of what the seller mentioned), and the fence is not the line (decades-old fences wander, and the survey — not memory, not the fence, not the listing's lot sketch — settles it). Most issues surface harmlessly in title review; the expensive ones surface after closing, mid-dispute. NV Ball Real Estate Group reads the title exceptions on every purchase and knows when a survey is cheap insurance — here's the field guide.

Read my title exceptions

Easements 101

Recorded easements — utilities, access, drainage, ditches (a Nevada classic) — appear in your title commitment's exceptions and bind you after closing. Most are benign; some shape what you can build or fence. We read every exception schedule and translate it before you're committed.

The fence-versus-line truth

Fences follow history, not surveys — and acreage especially wanders. When the line matters (building plans, disputes, big-value boundaries), a licensed survey settles it for a fraction of one lawyer-month. We'll tell you honestly when your purchase deserves one.

When disputes arrive

Neighbor encroachments, prescriptive-use claims, fence fights: document first (survey, photos, records), talk second (most resolve neighbor-to-neighbor with facts on the table), lawyer third. And disclosure: known disputes belong on a seller's form — surprises here unwind deals and worse.

Straight answers

What is an easement and does it transfer when I buy?

A recorded right over your land — utilities, access, drainage, ditches — that runs with the land and binds you after closing. It's in the title exceptions, which deserve a real read: most benign, some plan-shaping.

Is my fence the property line?

Unproven until surveyed: fences follow history, not law, and they wander. When the line matters, a licensed survey settles it affordably — and beats memory every time.

My neighbor's shed is over the line — what now?

Survey first, conversation second (facts beat accusations), attorney third. Don't let 'later' ripen into prescriptive claims — and if selling, disclose the known dispute.
