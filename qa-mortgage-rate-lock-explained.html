Rate locks explained: when to lock, honestly

A rate lock is your lender's commitment to hold a quoted rate for a set window — commonly from contract acceptance through closing — so market moves during escrow can't reprice your loan. The mechanics worth knowing: locks run for defined periods matched to your closing timeline (extensions exist and cost money, which is why escrow delays have a price), float-down options sometimes let a locked rate follow the market down once (terms vary by lender and deserve reading), and locking before you're in contract is generally premature. The honest part: nobody — not us, not your lender, not the forecasters — reliably knows where rates go next week, so the lock decision is about protecting a payment that works, not winning a prediction game. Here's the framework.

Talk timing with us

The mechanics

Locks attach to a defined period matched to your expected closing — the quote's lock length is part of the quote, and comparing lenders means comparing it too. Extensions exist at a cost (one honest reason to keep escrow on schedule), relocks after expiration follow current market, and the lock protects both directions: rates rising can't hurt you, and without a float-down, falling rates don't help.

Float-downs and the fine print

Some lenders offer float-down provisions — one chance for a locked rate to follow the market down if it moves enough — with thresholds, fees and windows that vary enough to deserve the actual document. Worth asking every lender you shop: the payment math is the point, and the lock's terms are part of the price.

The no-crystal-ball rule

The lock decision framework that doesn't need forecasts: if the current rate makes the payment work, locking protects a deal you already like; gambling an affordable payment on a prediction is how buyers lose houses they loved. We coordinate the lock conversation with your lender at contract — timing it to your escrow, not to headlines.

Straight answers

What is a mortgage rate lock and when should I lock?

A held rate for a set window, usually contract-to-closing. Lock when in contract and the payment works. Extensions cost; float-downs vary by lender; nobody predicts rates — protect the payment, skip the prediction game.

What happens if my rate lock expires before closing?

Extension fees or a market-rate relock — both worse than closing on time. Match the lock to a realistic timeline up front; run escrow with discipline.

Can my rate go down after I lock?

Only via a float-down where offered (one chance, thresholds, terms in writing). Otherwise the lock holds both ways; big post-closing drops become a refinance question.
